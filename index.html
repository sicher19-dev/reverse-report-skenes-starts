The Real Story

Pitching too fine, and paying for it in pitches

Efficiency Audit  // why 105 pitches for 5.1 innings

He kept trying to make hitters miss badly instead of just beating them

The stuff grade and the CSW say this was one of his best nights of the year. The pitch count says something else was going on: once he got a hitter to two strikes, he too often stopped attacking the zone and started hunting a chase — missing further off the plate instead of closer to it. That’s pitching fine (as in, too fine) rather than pitching to put hitters away.

Case Study: Ian Happ, three at-bats, one pattern

2nd inning (9 pitches): got to 1-2, then missed a sinker 1.1 ft off the plate and a fastball 1.8 ft off the plate trying to get a chase — full count before a slider finally froze him. He had Happ beat on pitch four; it took five more to finish him.

4th inning (6 pitches): same shape — up 1-2, missed a fastball 2.4 ft off the plate, came back for the punchout two pitches later.

6th inning (7 pitches): up 1-2 again, spiked a changeup well out of the zone chasing a whiff, eventually got the groundout on pitch seven.

The fix is simple: once he’s up two strikes on a hitter who’s shown he won’t chase off the plate, the next pitch should live at the edge of the zone, not well outside it. A competitive pitch that’s taken for a ball still sets up the next one; a pitch a foot off the plate just spends bullets. Happ took the free look every time and made him throw four extra pitches across three at-bats — over 10% of his pitch count on one hitter.

The Bottom Line

The stuff doesn't need fixing — the plan for two strikes does

This was, pitch for pitch, one of his best-executed starts of the year. The distance between that and a 5.1-inning, 105-pitch outing is entirely about what happens after he gets ahead. Tighten the two-strike chase pattern and this is a 7-inning, 90-pitch, 11-strikeout gem instead.

Keep

Everything about the stuff itself — 34.3% CSW is a season high, the fastball sat 96.3 with no in-game fade, and 11 strikeouts against a good lineup is elite. The two runs off his own pitches were earned contact, not mistakes.

Fix

The two-strike chase pattern. Twenty-one pitches thrown after already getting to two strikes is the entire gap between this outing and a complete-game-caliber pitch count. Attack the edge, not the dirt, once a hitter’s shown zone discipline.

The Ask  // bringing back the curveball

A new tool for exactly this problem — with one caveat

A show-me curveball is a well-matched answer to the two-strike chase problem: instead of missing a fastball or sinker a foot off the plate trying to induce a swing, a slow curve thrown for a competitive strike changes the hitter’s timing and can end at-bats on weak contact rather than a fifth or sixth pitch. It’s a real option worth building.

The caveat

He’s thrown a curveball twice all season, both in spring (3/26 and 4/1) — it hasn’t been part of the in-game mix since. This isn’t re-anchoring a faded weapon like the sweeper or slider; it’s introducing something close to new. It needs bullpen reps and a shape target before it sees a lineup — not a decision made mid-game.

The next step

Pull the shape from those two spring reps as a starting point, run it in the next bullpen alongside the sweeper/slider work already in progress, and set a target velo/shape before it’s trusted in a two-strike spot. If it earns a role, its job is specifically what tonight needed: a competitive, different-look strike to end at-bats that have gone past two strikes with no chase.

STANDING RULE — same as the fastball/slider work: nothing about the curveball counts until it's thrown for competitive strikes in bullpens first, then holds up in game at-bats.
